\title{Mutation-Guided Hallucination Repair in Large Language Models}

\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}

Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently produce plausible but incorrect natural-language responses and generated code, undermining their reliability in knowledge-intensive applications and software development. Many existing approaches either identify hallucinations without repairing them, rely on external evidence or model adaptation, or explore self-contained repair primarily through input-side perturbation and response consistency.
To address these limitations, we propose MutRepair, a self-contained framework that transforms output-space metamorphic mutations into a structured repair space.
Starting from an initial response, MutRepair constructs a set of metamorphic-relation-guided mutations, independently re-examines and repairs each mutation under an explicit fault assumption, and applies LLM-as-Judge Aggregation to the repaired candidates to select the final output. This unified workflow applies to both factual question answering and code generation through modality-specific mutation operators, requires no external knowledge, tools, or model retraining, and is compatible with both open- and closed-source LLMs.
Our evaluation across four representative LLMs, three factual question-answering benchmarks, and an executable code-generation benchmark demonstrates that MutRepair delivers the strongest overall repair performance among state-of-the-art baselines. On factual question answering, MutRepair achieves the lowest overall recheck hallucination rate (RHR) for every model, reducing RHR by $9.2\%\text{--}47.1\%$ relative to the strongest baseline while keeping its overall over-correction rate below $3\%$ on every model. On code generation, it achieves both the lowest RHR and the highest hallucination repair rate (HRR) for all four models, reducing RHR by $1.61\%\text{--}40.40\%$ relative to the strongest baseline and successfully repairing $22.3\%\text{--}44.9\%$ of initially incorrect programs. Repeated-run and sensitivity analyses further confirm that these gains remain stable across stochastic generations and variations in temperature and mutation count.
    
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs), such as the GPT series~\cite{openai2023gpt}, are now embedded as core components of software systems, powering tasks from open-domain question answering to code generation~\cite{huo2024large, champa2024chatgpt, jin2024simllm, lian2024imperfect}. Yet a critical reliability challenge persists: LLMs are prone to hallucinations, outputs that appear fluent and coherent yet are factually incorrect or unfaithful to ground truth~\cite{ji2023survey, xu2024hallucination}. Figure~\ref{fig:haluexample} shows such a hallucination from ChatGPT in the legal domain, where the model confidently asserts false information that could mislead users. Crucially, hallucinations are not confined to natural language: when an LLM generates code, the same failure surfaces as non-existent API calls, incorrect boundary conditions, or flawed logic that silently breaks the program~\cite{tian2025codehalu}. From a software engineering perspective, both forms constitute a single class of defect, a knowledge-intensive component emitting plausible but incorrect artifacts~\cite{fan2023llmse, zhang2023siren}, placing hallucination among classic SE concerns such as test oracle construction, automated testing, and automated program repair.

A growing body of work investigates hallucination detection~\cite{huo2023retrieving, yao2023llm}, yet detection alone is insufficient because users and downstream systems require reliable outputs rather than warnings. Recent approaches therefore increasingly target hallucination repair. Many rely on model fine-tuning~\cite{kumar2025training} or retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval, ayala2024reducing}, while self-contained methods such as DrHall~\cite{wu2025detecting} perturb the input question and exploit consistency among the resulting responses. These methods establish the value of inference-time repair, but output-space mutation remains underexplored as a mechanism for constructing and evaluating repair results.

Directly operating in the output space introduces a demanding oracle problem. Without external ground truth, a repair framework must construct diagnostically useful repair candidates and accurately judge the resulting repair outcomes using only the information available within the model. It must also avoid over-correction. Analogous to a regression-inducing patch in automated program repair~\cite{petke2024patch}, an unnecessary modification may corrupt an originally correct output and become as harmful as leaving a hallucination unrepaired. Reliable self-contained hallucination repair therefore depends on two essential capabilities: constructing an effective repair space and accurately evaluating repair outcomes without an external oracle.

In this paper, we present MutRepair, a self-contained hallucination repair framework that recasts hallucinations as repairable faults and integrates output-space metamorphic mutations, fault-assumption-guided self-repair, and LLM-as-Judge Aggregation. Our key insight is that a single response provides only one starting point for self-correction and may repeatedly lead the model toward the same erroneous conclusion. MutRepair instead transforms the initial response into a structured set of mutations, establishing multiple repair trajectories that re-express its content, reorganize its structure, or reimplement its solution strategy. Each mutation is then repaired independently, and the resulting repair outcomes are compared across mutations to support a more reliable final judgment.

MutRepair operates in three stages. \emph{(1) Mutation Construction} generates metamorphic-relation-guided mutations of the initial response to establish multiple complementary repair trajectories. \emph{(2) Fault-Assumption-Guided Self-Repair} introduces an explicit fault hypothesis for each mutation, prompting the model to determine whether a factual or logical fault is present and to repair the mutation only when a correction is warranted. \emph{(3) LLM-as-Judge Aggregation} performs cross-mutation comparison over the independently repaired candidates and identifies the most reliable final output without external ground truth. The same architecture operates on both natural-language responses and generated code through modality-specific mutation operators, demonstrating the effectiveness of mutation-guided repair across factual and executable artifacts.

We evaluate MutRepair across two modalities using three factual question-answering benchmarks, TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}, HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, and FreshQA~\cite{vu2023freshllms}, together with an executable code-generation benchmark constructed from LeetCode problems~\cite{xia2025leetcodedataset}. The evaluation covers four representative LLMs, including GPT-4o, GPT-5, Gemini, and Qwen3, and compares MutRepair with CoT~\cite{wei2022chain}, CoVe~\cite{dhuliawala2024chain}, its retrieval-augmented variant CoVe-SE, and the self-contained metamorphic repair method DrHall~\cite{wu2025detecting}. MutRepair delivers the strongest overall repair performance across both modalities. On factual question answering, it achieves the lowest overall RHR and OCR for every model, reducing RHR by $9.2\%\text{--}47.1\%$ relative to the strongest baseline while maintaining OCR below $3\%$. Paired McNemar tests confirm statistically significant RHR improvements in 11 of the 12 comparisons against the three self-contained baselines after Holm correction. On code generation, MutRepair achieves the lowest RHR and highest HRR for all four models, reducing RHR by $1.61\%\text{--}40.40\%$ relative to the strongest baseline and repairing $22.3\%\text{--}44.9\%$ of initially incorrect programs. Repeated-run and sensitivity analyses further demonstrate that these gains remain stable across stochastic generations, temperature settings, and mutation counts.

Our major contributions include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=20pt]
\item We propose MutRepair, a self-contained hallucination repair framework that combines output-space metamorphic mutation, fault-assumption-guided self-repair, and LLM-as-Judge Aggregation without external resources.

\item We show that MutRepair repairs both natural-language and code outputs within a shared framework, using three cross-modal mutation operators and an additional operator for code.

\item We evaluate MutRepair on three factual question-answering benchmarks and an executable code-generation benchmark across four LLMs, demonstrating consistently stronger repair performance, low over-correction, and robust results across repeated runs and sensitivity settings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
MutRepair is a self-contained hallucination repair framework that treats LLM-generated content as potentially faulty and repairs it without external knowledge or ground-truth feedback. Given an input $Q$ and its base response $B$, MutRepair processes $B$ through three stages, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}:

\textit{(1) Mutation Construction}, which transforms $B$ into a set of metamorphic-relation-guided mutations that establish complementary repair trajectories;

\textit{(2) Fault-Assumption-Guided Self-Repair}, which prompts the model to determine whether each mutation contains a factual or logical fault and repair it when necessary; and

\textit{(3) LLM-as-Judge Aggregation}, which compares the independently repaired candidates across mutations with respect to $Q$ and identifies the most reliable final output.

The same three-stage architecture applies to both natural-language responses and generated code. Stage~1 uses modality-specific mutation operators, while the self-repair and LLM-as-Judge Aggregation processes in Stages~2 and~3 remain shared across modalities and require no external oracle. Algorithm~\ref{alg:mt-repair-ranking} presents the complete procedure.

\subsection{Mutation Construction} \label{sec:mutation}

This stage is illustrated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:mt-repair-ranking} (lines~2--4). Given an input $Q$ and its base response $B$, MutRepair generates a set of structured mutations $M = \{m_1, m_2, \dots, m_k\}$ (line~3), where each mutation is derived from $B$ through a metamorphic-relation-guided transformation. These mutations expand a single response into multiple repair trajectories with different semantic or structural characteristics. Each mutation is independently repaired in Stage~2, and the resulting repair outcomes are compared across mutations in Stage~3.

A key design choice in MutRepair is to mutate the generated output rather than the input question. Input-space methods such as DrHall~\cite{wu2025detecting} perturb the input question and check whether the model's answers stay consistent across rephrasings. MutRepair instead generates mutations in the output space, directly transforming the base response and using each mutation as an independent starting point for re-examination and repair when necessary.

Metamorphic relations (MRs), originating from metamorphic testing, define how related executions should preserve or alter specific properties without requiring an explicit test oracle~\cite{chen2018metamorphic, zhang2014search, cho2025metamorphic}. MutRepair adapts MRs as structured mutation operators for repair rather than using them as pass-or-fail oracles. Each relation transforms the base response $B$ along a controlled semantic or structural dimension, creating a complementary repair trajectory whose outcome is evaluated after self-repair. Building on output-space MRs previously used for hallucination detection~\cite{yang2025hallucination}, we instantiate three cross-modal relations for natural-language responses and generated code.
\textbf{(1) Meaning-Preserving Rewrite} alters only the surface form of $B$: for text, it rephrases the wording (e.g., a synonymous predicate); for code, it substitutes an equivalent expression (e.g., \texttt{x += 1} for \texttt{x = x + 1}). 
\textbf{(2) Structural Transformation} reorganizes the structure of $B$ without changing its behavior: for text, it reorders the assertion (e.g., active--passive inversion); for code, it restructures the control or data flow (e.g., an \texttt{if}--\texttt{else} into a guard clause). 
\textbf{(3) Polarity Transformation} rewrites an assertion through double negation while preserving its original meaning. For text, it converts an affirmative statement into a logically equivalent double-negative form, or vice versa. For code, it introduces or removes paired logical negations in Boolean conditions (e.g., \texttt{if x:} and \texttt{if not not x:}). The intended equivalence concerns the condition's truth value, not arbitrary expression values or types. This transformation changes how the assertion or condition is expressed without reversing its semantics. Although logically equivalent to the original assertion, the transformed expression provides a distinct repair trajectory from which the model re-examines the same underlying proposition.

While these three relations apply to both modalities, code has an additional fault space that text does not: beyond surface form, faults arise from algorithmic and data-structural choices. We therefore introduce a fourth, code-specific operator: \textbf{(4) Algorithm/Data-Structure Variant}, which replaces the core algorithm or data structure of $B$ with a plausible alternative intended to solve the same task (e.g., recursion to iteration or memoization to tabulation), exposing faults invisible to surface-level rewrites. Figure~\ref{fig:codemut} illustrates this operator on a representative code mutation.

\subsection{Fault-Assumption-Guided Self-Repair}

Given the mutation set $M$, MutRepair repairs the potential fault carried by each mutation and produces a set of repaired candidates $A$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:mt-repair-ranking}, lines~5--9). This stage draws on two paradigms from software reliability engineering: fault injection~\cite{yu2025survey}, which deliberately introduces faults to expose system weaknesses, and automated program repair, which localizes and corrects defects. MutRepair transfers their diagnostic principles to model outputs at the prompt level. Rather than injecting an actual fault, it imposes an explicit fault assumption on each mutation and directs the model to identify and repair any error it contains.

This design targets a well-documented obstacle: when re-examining their own outputs, LLMs exhibit confirmation bias and tend to reaffirm, rather than critically reassess, their prior responses~\cite{pan2023automatically}, so simply re-prompting the model to ``check'' a response rarely surfaces its errors. Instead, we treat each mutation $m \in M$ as potentially faulty and inject an explicit fault assumption into the prompt (lines~7--8): the model is told the response may contain an error, such as a factual mistake in text or faulty logic, a non-existent API, or a boundary error in code, and is required to re-examine it, preserve it if correct, and otherwise return a corrected version.

The same fault-assumption-guided repair strategy is applied to both natural-language and code mutations, with modality-specific fault descriptions for factual and code outputs. The fault assumption serves as a diagnostic instruction rather than evidence that a mutation is incorrect. The original question anchors each repair: the model is prompted to reassess the candidate against the task requirements, rather than treating the mutation itself as a reason to change the answer. This shifts the model from passive validation into active fault localization and repair, eliciting deeper self-scrutiny than plain regeneration or rephrasing. Repairing all mutations independently yields a set of repaired candidates $A$ (line~9), which are passed to Stage~3, LLM-as-Judge Aggregation.

\subsection{LLM-as-Judge Aggregation}\label{sec:llmasjudge}

After fault-assumption-guided self-repair, MutRepair obtains a set of independently repaired candidates $A$, each derived from a different mutation of the base response (Algorithm~\ref{alg:mt-repair-ranking}, lines~10--20). Because these candidates may still differ in correctness and quality, the final stage applies LLM-as-Judge Aggregation to identify the most reliable output. Functionally, this stage plays the role of patch validation: multiple repaired candidates are assessed with respect to the original input, and only the highest-ranked candidate is accepted. Recent studies~\cite{li2025generation, zeng2024llm} similarly demonstrate that LLMs can effectively compare candidate outputs without external resources.

We adopt a prompt-based pairwise ranking strategy to aggregate the repaired candidates. Under the fixed ranking prompt $P_r$, the LLM receives the original input $Q$ together with the complete candidate set $A$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:mt-repair-ranking} describes the comparison procedure specified by the ranking prompt. It first determines the number of repaired candidates and initializes the ranking matrix $R$ (lines~11--12). It then enumerates every candidate pair $a_i$ and $a_j$ (lines~13--14). For each pair, the prompt directs the LLM to assess $a_i$ relative to $a_j$ according to correctness and completeness with respect to $Q$. The resulting assessment is recorded as $R_{i,j}$, where a lower value indicates a stronger candidate (lines~15--16). Although the algorithm presents these comparisons individually, all candidates are included in the same ranking context and the complete pairwise-ranking process is performed under a single ranking prompt. We quantify the resulting computational cost in Section~\ref{sec:6.2}.

The total score of each candidate is obtained by summing its row in $R$ (lines~17--18). The candidate with the lowest total score is therefore the one that performs best across all pairwise comparisons and is returned as the final output $A^*$ (lines~19--20). Unlike absolute confidence scoring, this comparative formulation evaluates each candidate against the available repair alternatives and avoids requiring the LLM to calibrate an isolated correctness score.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Research Questions}
We formulate the following research questions to systematically evaluate the effectiveness, internal mechanisms, and robustness of MutRepair. 

\textbf{RQ1.} How does MutRepair perform in repairing hallucinations compared to baselines?

\textbf{RQ2.} How do the key components of MutRepair contribute to its hallucination repair performance? 

\textbf{RQ3.} How robust is MutRepair to LLM stochasticity, and how sensitive is it to key parameters such as temperature and the number of mutations?

RQ1 evaluates whether MutRepair can effectively repair hallucinations and outperform state-of-the-art baselines, while simultaneously controlling the risk of over-correction.  
RQ2 investigates the internal mechanisms of MutRepair by analyzing the effects of different aggregation strategies and isolating the contributions of mutation-guided repair, as well as characterizing the remaining upper-bound repair potential.  
RQ3 examines the stability and robustness of MutRepair under repeated runs and studies its sensitivity to key generation parameters, in order to understand how stochasticity and parameter choices influence repair effectiveness and safety.


\subsection{Baselines}

We compare MutRepair against state-of-the-art self-contained baselines that require no external resources such as knowledge bases or retrieval systems, covering three complementary repair strategies: internal reasoning, self-verification, and metamorphic-relation-guided repair. \textbf{Chain-of-Thought (CoT)}~\cite{wei2022chain} prompts the LLM to reason step by step before producing its final answer, eliciting its latent reasoning ability; it applies to both natural-language and code tasks. \textbf{Chain-of-Verification (CoVe)}~\cite{dhuliawala2024chain} extracts key claims from an initial response, generates verification questions, answers them with the LLM itself, and feeds the results back to revise the answer; we apply it to natural-language tasks. \textbf{DrHall}~\cite{wu2025detecting} is a recent software-engineering method that, like MutRepair, leverages metamorphic testing to repair hallucinations across both modalities; the key distinction is that DrHall constructs variants of the input question and checks answer consistency across them. We adopt its best-performing configuration that requires no external knowledge, ensuring a fair comparison under our self-contained setting.

As a non-self-contained reference, \textbf{CoVe-SE}~\cite{dhuliawala2024chain} is a variant of CoVe that answers the verification questions with a search engine rather than the LLM, serving as a representative retrieval-augmented (RAG-style) baseline.

We note that several recent methods extend verification beyond CoVe~\cite{wu2024large, song2025progco, he2024retrieving}. We do not adopt them as primary baselines because ProCo requires identifying a maskable key condition in each question, ProgCo's published verification prompts target instruction constraints and explicit mathematical solution steps and would require task-specific verifier designs for our factual answers and generated programs, while CoV-RAG relies on external retrieval and training. In addition, detection-only methods such as SelfCheckGPT~\cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} and MetaQA~\cite{yang2025hallucination} fall outside our scope, as they identify hallucinations without producing a corrected output. Code-specific program-repair systems commonly rely on execution feedback, compiler diagnostics, failing tests, or repository context, which are unavailable under our self-contained setting. We therefore compare MutRepair with repair methods that operate solely through the target LLM without access to such external signals.


\subsection{Datasets}
We evaluate MutRepair across two modalities. For natural language, we adopt three widely used benchmarks that cover complementary hallucination scenarios: TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa} for misconception- and bias-driven hallucinations, HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa} for reasoning-induced hallucinations, and a stable-answer subset of FreshQA~\cite{vu2023freshllms} for factual reliability. For code, we adopt LeetCodeDataset~\cite{xia2025leetcodedataset} to assess whether the same framework generalizes to code-generation hallucinations.

\subsubsection{TruthfulQA}
We use the full set of 817 questions across 38 categories (e.g., health, law, finance), which are designed to elicit responses influenced by widespread misconceptions and false beliefs.

\subsubsection{HotpotQA}
HotpotQA poses multi-hop questions that require reasoning over multiple supporting facts. From its 113K questions, we randomly sample 610; following statistical sampling theory with finite population correction, this yields a 99\% confidence level at an approximately 5.2\% margin of error under $p=0.5$.

\subsubsection{FreshQA}
FreshQA targets hallucinations arising from outdated or missing knowledge. To ensure fair evaluation across model training cutoffs, we retain 399 questions whose answers were established before 2024 and remain stable over time, excluding rapidly evolving queries in favor of those fixed by authoritative sources. For example, questions such as ``Which country will host the most matches in the 2026 FIFA World Cup?'' refer to information that has already been fixed by official schedules; therefore, the correct answer (the United States) is not expected to change. This reduces ambiguity in ground-truth labeling by focusing on questions with stable factual states.

\subsubsection{LeetCode}
LeetCode Dataset~\cite{xia2025leetcodedataset} is a collection of Python programming problems, each paired with a specification and starter code. We uniformly sample 200 problems at random from the full dataset, without stratification or filtering by problem category or difficulty. The resulting sample naturally contains 41 Easy, 100 Medium, and 59 Hard problems.

In total, our evaluation comprises 1,826 natural-language questions across the three QA benchmarks and 200 code-generation problems.

\subsection{Studied LLMs}

To ensure a representative evaluation, we assess four LLMs selected from LiveBench~\cite{white2025livebench}, covering different providers, model generations, and access modes. We include GPT-4o (gpt-4o)~\cite{hurst2024gpt} and GPT-5 (gpt-5)~\cite{singh2025openai} from OpenAI to examine whether MutRepair remains effective across two generations of the same model family. Gemini (gemini-2.5-flash-thinking)~\cite{comanici2025gemini} from Google provides cross-provider coverage, while Qwen3 (qwen3-32b)~\cite{yang2025qwen3} allows us to assess whether MutRepair remains effective on an open-source model.

Unless otherwise specified, all models use a temperature of $0.1$ to introduce mild stochasticity while preserving stability. All dataset sampling procedures use a fixed random seed of 42, while all other decoding parameters follow the default settings of the corresponding APIs or serving frameworks. All experiments are conducted on a server equipped with two Intel Xeon Gold 6354C processors and two NVIDIA L40s 48 GB GPUs. We use the official Ollama~\cite{ollama2026} release of Qwen3, while GPT-5, GPT-4o, and Gemini are accessed through their respective chat-completion APIs.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate MutRepair and all baseline approaches using three complementary metrics: recheck hallucination rate, hallucination repair rate, and over-correction rate. Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the evaluation dataset. For each input $Q_i \in \mathcal{D}$, let $B_i$ denote the base response generated by the LLM and $A_i$ denote the final repaired output. Let $h(\cdot)$ be an indicator function such that $h(x)=1$ if output $x$ is classified as hallucinated under the modality-specific oracle and $h(x)=0$ otherwise.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=10pt]

\item \textbf{Recheck hallucination rate (RHR)} measures the overall proportion of hallucinated outputs remaining after applying a repair approach, capturing the final reliability of the system after intervention:
\begin{equation}
\textbf{RHR} =
\frac{\left|\{\, i \mid Q_i \in \mathcal{D} \land h(A_i)=1 \,\}\right|}
{\left| \mathcal{D} \right|}.
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Hallucination repair rate (HRR)} measures the proportion of originally hallucinated responses that are successfully repaired into correct outputs, directly reflecting an approach's ability to fix hallucinations:
\begin{equation}
\textbf{HRR} =
\frac{\left|\{\, i \mid Q_i \in \mathcal{D} \land h(B_i)=1 \land h(A_i)=0 \,\}\right|}
{\left| \{\, i \mid Q_i \in \mathcal{D} \land h(B_i)=1\} \right|}.
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Over-correction rate (OCR)} measures the proportion of originally correct responses that become hallucinated after repair. This metric is critical for assessing repair safety, as false repairs can be as harmful as unresolved hallucinations:
\begin{equation}
\textbf{OCR} =
\frac{\left|\{\, i \mid Q_i \in \mathcal{D} \land h(B_i)=0 \land h(A_i)=1 \,\}\right|}
{\left| \{\, i \mid Q_i \in \mathcal{D} \land h(B_i)=0\} \right|}.
\end{equation}

\end{itemize}

The three metrics above are modality-agnostic; they depend only on the hallucination indicator $h(\cdot)$, whose determination differs between the two modalities. For each model and method, the overall natural-language metrics are computed by combining the sample counts across the three benchmarks and applying the corresponding metric formulas, rather than averaging the benchmark-level percentages.

For \textbf{code}, we follow the definition of code hallucination in CodeHalu~\cite{tian2025codehalu} and use the executable unit tests associated with each problem as the oracle. Both base and repaired programs are labeled as non-hallucinated if they pass all unit tests and hallucinated otherwise. The tests are used solely for evaluation and are never exposed to MutRepair during repair, preserving the self-contained setting.

For \textbf{natural language}, no executable oracle exists, so we follow the two-stage response-verification strategy used in MetaQA~\cite{yang2025hallucination}, combining LLM-assisted pre-screening with manual review of responses not resolved automatically. Each question is paired with a reference bank of correct and incorrect answers. We use GPT-5.1 (gpt-5.1), a frontier model at the time of evaluation, to automatically label only responses that it judges semantically equivalent to an answer in the reference bank. All remaining responses are independently reviewed by two authors, with all method identifiers removed to ensure that the reviewers are blinded to the approach that generated each response. Initial disagreements, computed as the number of outputs receiving conflicting labels divided by the total number of evaluated outputs, account for $0.5\%$ and are resolved through discussion in a second review round. The same pre-screening and blinded-review protocol is applied to outputs from every method.

\subsection{Components Analysis (RQ2)}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:llmasjudge}, MutRepair applies LLM-as-Judge Aggregation, implemented through pairwise ranking, to six repaired candidates: the repaired base response and five repaired mutations. We examine whether pairwise ranking outperforms two alternatives commonly used in prior work~\cite{li2025generation}, namely majority voting and confidence-based selection, and whether each component contributes to the final repair quality. We additionally report pass@6, an oracle upper bound that counts an instance as repairable if at least one of the six candidates is correct, to quantify the remaining headroom in candidate selection.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/rq2.1.pdf}
    \caption{Hallucination repair performance under different LLM-as-Judge Aggregation strategies.}
    \label{fig:rq2.1}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Effect of aggregation strategies}
We compare pairwise ranking against two aggregation strategies widely used in prior work. \textbf{Majority voting} selects the candidate whose meaning is semantically closest to the largest number of other candidates. \textbf{Confidence-based selection} prompts the model to attach a self-estimated confidence score to each candidate and keeps the highest-scoring one.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2.1}, Pairwise Ranking consistently achieves the highest hallucination repair rate (HRR) across all four LLMs, while simultaneously attaining the lowest recheck hallucination rate (RHR) and the lowest over-correction rate (OCR). The HRR gains over the stronger of the two baselines are most pronounced on GPT-5, where HRR rises from 56.59\% to 61.54\%, and the RHR and OCR advantages hold throughout: on GPT-5, ranking lowers RHR to 7.89\% (versus 10.08\% and 9.15\%) and OCR to 0.27\% (versus 0.48\% and 0.62\%).
These results indicate that LLM-as-Judge Aggregation is more effective than majority voting and confidence-based selection: by explicitly modeling the relative correctness of repaired candidates rather than their frequency or self-reported confidence, pairwise ranking repairs more hallucinations while introducing fewer errors into originally correct answers. We therefore adopt it as MutRepair's default aggregation strategy.


\subsubsection{Ablation on mutation-guided repair}
To isolate each component's contribution to repairing initially hallucinated responses, we compare full MutRepair against three ablated versions using HRR: (1) \emph{without mutations}, which drops the mutation stage entirely and repairs the base response in a single pass; (2) \emph{without MR}, which retains the same number of candidates and aggregation strategy but replaces the MR operators with five independently sampled generic restatements of the base response; and (3) \emph{without fault assumption}, which keeps the mutations but removes the explicit fault assumption from the self-repair prompt. The \emph{without MR} condition uses the same temperature ($T=0.1$) as full MutRepair, and neither condition applies an additional candidate-deduplication filter.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/rq2.2.pdf}
    \caption{Effects of mutations and fault-assumption guidance on MutRepair's performance.}
    \label{fig:rq2.2}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2.2}, full MutRepair achieves the strongest overall HRR, outperforming or matching the ablated configurations in 35 of the 36 model--dataset--ablation comparisons. Removing the mutation stage reduces the mean HRR across the twelve model--dataset settings from 53.81\% to 45.84\%. On GPT-5--HotpotQA, for example, HRR falls from 29.91\% to 15.38\%, showing that multiple mutation-guided repair trajectories improve upon single-pass self-repair. Replacing the MR operators with generic restatements reduces the mean HRR to 42.62\% and underperforms full MutRepair in eleven of the twelve settings. This result shows that, under the same candidate count and temperature, MR-guided mutations are more effective than generic restatements. However, because neither condition enforces candidate uniqueness, this comparison does not fully separate the effect of MR guidance from that of candidate diversity. Removing the fault assumption produces the largest and most consistent decline: the mean HRR falls to 36.59\%, and HRR decreases in all twelve settings. The decrease is particularly pronounced on GPT-5--TruthfulQA, where HRR falls from 80.19\% to 45.13\%. This systematic result is consistent with~\cite{yang2025confidence}, which identifies self-critique as a central factor in successful self-correction.

We additionally compare MutRepair with pass@6 to measure how much candidate-level repair potential is recovered by LLM-as-Judge Aggregation. MutRepair remains within 5 percentage points of the oracle upper bound in nine of the twelve settings, including GPT-4o--TruthfulQA, where it achieves 87.97\% HRR compared with 90.37\% for pass@6. The largest gap occurs on GPT-5--FreshQA, where MutRepair achieves 54.29\% HRR compared with the pass@6 upper bound of 74.29\%. This gap indicates that correct repaired candidates are available but not always selected, identifying candidate judgment as an important direction for further improvement.

\mybox{\textbf{Answer to RQ2: }
Among the aggregation strategies, pairwise ranking achieves the highest HRR with the lowest RHR and OCR across all four models. Ablation shows that mutation construction, MR guidance, and the fault assumption each contribute to repair effectiveness, with full MutRepair outperforming or matching the ablated configurations in 35 of the 36 comparisons. MutRepair remains within 5 percentage points of the pass@6 upper bound in most settings, while the remaining gaps identify candidate judgment as an opportunity for further improvement.
}


\subsection{Stability and Robustness (RQ3)}

Beyond effectiveness, an important concern for a fully LLM-based hallucination repair framework is robustness: whether MutRepair remains stable under the inherent stochasticity of LLMs, and whether its performance is sensitive to key generation parameters. We study this along three axes: (1) the run-to-run stability of MutRepair under repeated execution, and its sensitivity to the two parameters that most directly govern candidate generation, namely (2) the sampling temperature and (3) the number of mutations. For the stability analysis, we conduct four complete executions of MutRepair on all $1826$ instances from the three natural-language benchmarks for each of the four studied models. These executions comprise the original RQ1 experiment and three additional repetitions under identical settings. The two parameter-sensitivity analyses are conducted on GPT-4o using a fixed sample of $180$ instances obtained by randomly sampling approximately $10\%$ of each natural-language benchmark. We do not combine code-generation instances with the natural-language benchmarks because they use different evaluation oracles.

\subsubsection{Impact of temperature}
Since MutRepair relies on LLMs not only to generate repairs but also to assess and select among candidates, its effectiveness may be sensitive to the sampling temperature, which prior studies have shown to substantially affect the stability and correctness of LLM outputs~\cite{arora2024optimizing, renze2024effect}. We therefore evaluate MutRepair on the 10\%-sampled dataset under four temperature settings $T=\{0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9\}$; Table~\ref{tab:rq3.2} reports the results.

Increasing the temperature does not noticeably improve repair effectiveness, with HRR remaining within a narrow range of $64.56\%\text{--}65.82\%$. The over-correction rate, however, rises from $2.97\%$ at $T{=}0.1$ to $8.91\%$ at $T{=}0.6$, an almost threefold increase. This result indicates that higher temperatures mainly increase the risk of over-correction rather than improving repair.

\subsubsection{Number of mutations}
Generating more mutations can expand the repair space, but it also increases the computational cost of MutRepair. We therefore evaluate MutRepair on the same sampled dataset with varying mutation counts $N=\{0,1,3,5,7,9\}$.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:rq3.3}, increasing the number of mutations yields clear but quickly saturating gains. The hallucination repair rate rises from $55.70\%$ at $N{=}0$ to $64.56\%$ at $N{=}5$, but improves only marginally thereafter, reaching $67.09\%$ at $N{=}9$; the recheck hallucination rate decreases overall from $22.22\%$ at $N{=}0$ to $16.67\%$ at $N{=}9$, despite a small rebound at $N{=}7$. Token consumption, in contrast, grows linearly at roughly $419$ tokens per additional mutation, so the token cost per percentage-point HRR gain relative to $N{=}0$ rises: improving HRR by one point costs about $82$ tokens at $N{=}1$ but around $258$--$331$ tokens beyond $N{=}5$. We therefore adopt $N{=}5$ as the default, which captures most of the attainable repair gains at a favorable cost-effectiveness ratio; the overall token cost is analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:6.2}.

\mybox{\textbf{Answer to RQ3: }
MutRepair is robust to LLM stochasticity, with standard deviations below $2$ percentage points for RHR, HRR, and OCR across four full-data executions on all four models. Its repair effectiveness is relatively insensitive to temperature, though higher temperatures raise the over-correction risk. The benefit of additional mutations saturates quickly, so we adopt $N{=}5$ to balance effectiveness against cost.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Case Study}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/casestudycode.pdf}
    \caption{MutRepair vs. DrHall on a code hallucination example.}
    \label{fig:casestudycode}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Code.} Figure~\ref{fig:casestudycode} shows a LeetCode binary-search example whose base response contains an off-by-one bug (\texttt{while lo < hi}) that fails when the target is the last element. DrHall mutates the \emph{question}, but rephrasing leaves the task unchanged, causing the model to regenerate the same bug. MutRepair instead mutates the \emph{response} and reimplements the search using \texttt{bisect\_left}. By changing the implementation strategy rather than merely rephrasing the task, MutRepair establishes an alternative repair trajectory that avoids the erroneous loop condition without access to unit tests. Fault-assumption-guided self-repair and LLM-as-Judge Aggregation then produce and select the correct implementation. The same output-space mutate-then-repair process thus handles both factual and code hallucinations.

\noindent\textbf{Natural language.} Figure~\ref{fig:casestudy} contrasts CoVe and MutRepair on the question ``Legal tender laws in the US require all businesses to accept what?'', whose base response wrongly claims cashless businesses are illegal. CoVe answers its verification questions using the LLM itself, but the verification answers repeat the same misconception and, folded back into revision, entrench the original error. MutRepair uses no external evidence: it constructs response-level mutations that restate the underlying claim, repairs each under an explicit fault assumption, and ranks the candidates, converging on the correct answer that ``no U.S.\ law compels businesses to accept cash''.

\noindent\textbf{Failure Mode.} We manually analyzed the unsuccessful repair outcomes and identified three modes. The dominant mode is failure to recover the required factual knowledge ($>75\%$ of the analyzed failures), where none of the generated candidates provides a correct answer. This finding highlights a fundamental assumption of self-contained repair: the correct knowledge must already be encoded in the model's parametric knowledge and remain recoverable through re-examination. MutRepair can reactivate such latent knowledge by establishing alternative repair trajectories, but no self-contained method can supply knowledge that the model does not possess. Other failures stem from imperfect pairwise judgment ($<5\%$ of the analyzed failures), where the ranking fails to select an existing correct candidate, and from prompt-induced over-correction, where the fault assumption occasionally leads the model to alter an already correct answer.


\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{sec:6.2}

As MutRepair generates multiple mutations and aggregates their repaired candidates, it consumes more tokens than a single forward pass. Although pairwise ranking considers every candidate pair, all comparisons are performed within a single prompt and therefore require only one LLM call rather than one call for each pair. The relevant question, however, is how this overhead compares to other repair methods rather than to a bare generation. Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the average token cost per QA process across the four models.

CoT issues a single prompt and is therefore by far the cheapest, but this lightweight design also limits its repair ability, and its repair rate is correspondingly low. All other methods rely on multiple LLM calls, and among them MutRepair is cost-competitive across all four models: its token cost is comparable to or lower than DrHall and CoVe on every model, and far below the retrieval-augmented CoVe-SE; the gap is largest on GPT-5, where MutRepair uses $1804.96$ tokens against CoVe's $6743.49$, a $73.2\%$ reduction. Overall, MutRepair attains the strongest repair performance while remaining competitive in token cost with the multi-call baselines.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
Hallucination repair rests on a normative notion of ``factual correctness'' that is not always clear-cut: some questions are subjective, under-determined by evidence, or socially contested, and for them the line between a hallucination and a legitimate interpretation is genuinely blurry. A repair method that returns an overly confident answer in such cases risks crowding out legitimate uncertainty. MutRepair instead instructs the model to preserve a candidate when no error is found during self-repair rather than forcing a change, and we do not claim to adjudicate contested questions of truth.


\section{Threats to Validity}

\subsection{Internal Validity}
The main internal threat is the residual gap to the pass@6 oracle, which shows that the aggregation stage does not always select a correct candidate when one exists. This is a limitation of the current LLM-as-Judge Aggregation implementation rather than of mutation-guided repair itself. Other implementation-level factors, such as occasional low-diversity mutations or imperfect pairwise judgments, may add noise to individual cases but are unlikely to bias the results systematically, since all methods are evaluated using the same base responses, datasets, metrics, and evaluation oracles. At the evaluation level, although the two authors were blinded to method identity, their judgments may still introduce bias into the reported natural-language metrics.

\subsection{External Validity}
The main external threat is the limited scope of our benchmarks. Although they cover complementary factual scenarios and code problems across different difficulty levels, they focus on short-form factual questions and standalone Python programs. Benchmark difficulty and discriminative power can also affect the assessment of model capabilities~\cite{zhou2025lost}. Further evaluation is needed for long-form generation, other programming languages, and repository-level software tasks.


\section{Related Work}

% LLM Hallucination Detection and Mitigation
Hallucination detection has been studied extensively, through comparison against external knowledge sources~\cite{guo2022survey, galitsky2025truth}, token-level uncertainty signals~\cite{varshney2023stitch,vazhentsev2025token}, LM-vs-LM cross-examination~\cite{cohen2023lm}, and self-detection via natural-language reasoning~\cite{zhao2024knowing}. Detection alone, however, only flags unreliable outputs rather than correcting them, which motivates the more recent line of hallucination repair.

% LLM Hallucination Repair
Repair methods aim to transform hallucinated outputs into reliable ones, but many follow the same external-signal paradigm as detection, drawing on external knowledge bases or retrieval systems~\cite{li2025rac}, auxiliary LLM agents~\cite{kwartler2024good}, or search engines and web resources~\cite{vladika2025correcting}.

% LLM Hallucination Self-correction
Self-correction asks whether LLMs can repair their own errors without external supervision. Reflexion uses task feedback to guide subsequent trials~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}, while Self-Refine iteratively revises an output using feedback from the same LLM~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}. Other studies examine the limits of intrinsic self-correction~\cite{huang2023large, tyen2024llms} and improvements through training~\cite{kumar2025training} or inference-time verification~\cite{wu2024large}. Unlike sequential critique-and-revision, MutRepair independently repairs multiple MR-guided mutations and aggregates their outcomes.

% SE approaches for LLM reliability
A growing body of work adapts software engineering methodologies, such as metamorphic testing and fault analysis, to LLM reliability. Some use metamorphic relations to detect or repair hallucinations by perturbing input questions~\cite{wu2025detecting}, generating logic-based test cases~\cite{li2024drowzee}, or transforming output responses~\cite{yang2025hallucination}. ProgCo~\cite{song2025progco} instead uses LLM-generated and LLM-executed verification pseudo-programs for self-correction; its published evaluation focuses on instruction-following and mathematical reasoning tasks.

% Summary
MutRepair advances this line of work by targeting output-level repair in a fully self-contained setting, requiring no retrieval, tools, or external agents. It uses structured metamorphic mutations to expand the repair space, assumes potential faults, and drives the model to actively repair them.


\section{Conclusions}
In this paper, we presented MutRepair, a self-contained framework for actively repairing hallucinations in LLM-generated natural-language and code outputs. Inspired by metamorphic relations and automated repair paradigms from software engineering, MutRepair systematically constructs metamorphic response mutations, performs fault-assumption-guided self-repair, and applies LLM-as-Judge Aggregation to select the final output. Extensive experiments across four representative LLMs on both natural-language and code-generation tasks demonstrate that MutRepair achieves the strongest overall balance between repair effectiveness and over-correction among the evaluated baselines.
